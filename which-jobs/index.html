Which manual jobs are worth automating?

The ones somebody uses, that mostly go straight through, and that would give back enough hours to be worth changing how people work. Most of the time the fix is a tool you buy or one set up properly, not new software. I build only when one job takes at least 15 to 20 hours a week by hand.

Five kinds of manual work

I sort every job done by hand into five kinds, and the kind sets the share I count as coming off. The free calculator uses the same shares: my judgment, kept cautious, not measurement.

  1. Typing in what arrives

    Orders typed in from email. The fix is one way in, such as a form.

    I count 75%.

  2. Copying between systems

    Invoices keyed from the job system into accounting. The fix is making the two talk.

    I count 70%.

  3. Chasing people

    Friday emails asking for timesheets. The fix is automatic reminders.

    I count 70%.

  4. Same facts, several places

    A customer's address in three places. The fix is one place allowed to be right.

    I count 50%.

  5. Judgment

    Quotes and drawing checks. A tool only makes the person quicker; I never build for it.

    I count 40%.

If nobody uses what a job produces, stop doing it. The whole job comes back at no cost.

How many go straight through

At least half have to, or automating won't work. Count out of ten how many need no phone call, chase or exception. At eight to ten, the kind's share counts in full; at five to seven, I count 70% to 90% of it; under five, fix the input first with one way in.

Buy a tool, set one up, leave it alone, or build

The kind of work and the hours decide which.

  • Buy a tool.For typing in what arrives, chasing people and judgment. In the report, I name the product and take no cut.
  • Set one up.For copying and same-facts work: a fixed price from me, or your own admin can do it with the same plan.
  • Leave it alone.Under 0.75 hours a week off. Copying and same-facts jobs under 1.5 are not first.
  • Build.Only past the build line at the top, with the hours counted rather than guessed, and most weeks have none. A build costs a tenth of what it takes off in its first year.
